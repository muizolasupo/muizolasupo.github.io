Nigeria's Reforms Got the Numbers Right. Why Haven't Nigerians Felt It Yet?

Nigeria's reforms have lifted growth, slowed inflation, rebuilt reserves and raised government revenue. Yet about 140 million Nigerians are poor, and wages buy less than they did. This piece explains why ordinary citizens have not felt the gains, where the money went, and what the January 2027 election should decide.

By Muiz Olasupo · 24 October 2026 · 6 min read · views

A stable economy, a strained household

Nigeria's reforms have got the big numbers right. The economy is growing faster and prices are rising more slowly. The Central Bank holds more foreign currency, the reserves that steady the naira and pay for imports, and governments are collecting far more revenue (NBS; NBS; World Bank; CBN). Yet ordinary Nigerians have not felt it.

Take, for instance, the petrol pump: how many litres a minimum-wage worker's monthly pay can buy. In July 2024, when the ₦70,000 minimum wage was set, petrol averaged about ₦770 a litre nationwide, so a month's pay bought about 91 litres (NBS). By May 2026 petrol averaged about ₦1,596, and the same pay bought about 44 (NBS). In under two years, the minimum wage has lost more than half its buying power at the pump.

Poverty tells the same story. Nigeria's poverty line is the income a person needs to afford basic food and other essentials. Measured against it, the World Bank estimates the poverty rate at about 63 percent in 2025, some 140 million people, up from 56 percent in 2023 (World Bank, April 2026). Nigeria has repaired the nation's books. Household budgets are still in the red.

Why growth has not reached households

Growth of about 4 percent sounds healthy, but Nigeria's population is also growing fast, so the gain for the average person is small. The World Bank itself calls growth per person weak (World Bank, October 2026). Most of the growth has also come from services and industry. Farming, the main source of work for more than half of poor Nigerians, has lagged behind, so the gains have largely missed the poor (World Bank, April 2026).

Prices are the bigger barrier. Lower inflation means prices are rising more slowly, not that they have fallen. With inflation at about 15 percent, goods that cost ₦10,000 a year ago cost about ₦11,500 or more today. Food hurts most. Staple food prices faced by the poor rose about fivefold between 2020 and 2024, so food that cost ₦1,000 in 2020 cost about ₦5,000 by 2024, and poor households spend up to 70 percent of their income on food (World Bank, October 2025; World Bank). Now the world oil price has climbed to $110 a barrel, and energy prices in Nigeria are 10.85 percent higher than a year ago (CNBC; NBS). Dearer oil brings the government more revenue, but households pay more for fuel and transport.

Extreme poverty shows the same pattern. The World Bank counts people living on less than $3 a day, adjusted for what money buys in Nigeria, as extremely poor. On its forecast, that share will still be higher in 2028 than it was in 2023.

Where the money went

Before 2023, the government held down the price of petrol by paying part of it, and it kept an official exchange rate that valued the naira far above its market price. Both quietly drained public money. Ending the petrol subsidy and letting the naira trade at a single market rate turned that hidden support into government revenue. After adjusting for inflation, revenue shared by the federal, state and local governments rose 69 percent between 2023 and 2025, and state revenues rose about 93 percent. The states spent most of the windfall on building: roads, buildings and other projects rose from 46 to 61 percent of their budgets. Cash transfers and other direct support for poor households got only 4.4 percent (World Bank).

That is the gap. Households paid the cost at once, through higher prices for fuel, transport and food. The dividend, the extra money the reforms raised, is taking the long road: through roads and buildings that raise incomes slowly, and only if they are finished. Protection for the poor should have come before the shock, not years after it.

The election test

Nigerians will vote for a president on 16 January 2027 (INEC), and the fuel subsidy is the sharpest dividing line. President Bola Tinubu has ruled out bringing it back: "You cannot ask Moses to go back to Egypt" (Channels TV).

Two leading challengers, Atiku Abubakar and Peter Obi, have said they will bring the subsidy back. Atiku has repeatedly promised to restore it, now as a "production subsidy": crude oil sold to Nigerian refineries at a preferential price, so that only fuel refined in Nigeria benefits. Obi has said, "By removing the corruption, I assure you that we will bring back subsidy," but has not said what form it would take. Seyi Makinde stops short of promising a subsidy, but he asks why crude supplied to Nigerian refineries should be "priced as though Nigeria does not produce crude oil." None of the three has said what his plan would cost (Vanguard).

The test for any plan is its design, not its label. A blanket pump subsidy pays most to those who burn most. A lower price for everyone gives the biggest savings to the people who buy the most fuel, usually car owners and the better-off. Selling crude cheaply to refiners also means the government earns less on every barrel, money it would otherwise share with the states.

Voters should put three questions to every candidate:

  1. How will you get cash to poor households quickly? Rising prices hit the poor first and hardest. A cash transfer goes straight to the families who need it, while a pump subsidy is spread across everyone who buys fuel, rich or poor. Yet states gave only 4.4 percent of their budgets to this kind of direct support.
  2. How will you bring down food prices? Poor households spend up to 70 percent of their income on food, so food prices matter more to them than any other number. A serious answer deals with what drives those prices: how much farmers can grow, and what it costs to store food and move it to market.
  3. How will you make states account for what they built with the windfall? State revenue nearly doubled after inflation, and most of the extra money went into roads, buildings and other projects. Voters should know what was built, what it cost and whether it was finished, because an unfinished project raises no one's income.

From stabilisation to development

Stabilisation, meaning calmer prices, a steadier naira and healthier public finances, is a precondition for development, not a substitute for it. Three to four million young Nigerians enter the labour market every year, about one in four young people is in neither work, education nor training, and more than 86 million Nigerians lack electricity (World Bank Country Partnership Framework). None of these numbers moves because reserves rise.

Cheaper money has not reached borrowers yet either. On 22 September the Central Bank cut its main interest rate, which guides what banks charge on loans, from 26.5 percent to 23 percent, its biggest cut since 2006 (CBN; The Guardian). A rate cut is meant to make loans cheaper, so that firms borrow, invest and hire. Instead, credit to businesses and households fell by ₦537.6 billion in September, from ₦84.5 trillion to ₦83.9 trillion (The Guardian). The cut came late in the month, so it is early days. But banks must still keep 45 percent of their deposits at the Central Bank, money they cannot lend out (CBN). A lower rate does little while so much of the banks' money stays locked away.

The reforms have given Nigeria's governments more money to spend and time to put it to good use. Whoever wins in January inherits the dividend. The test is whether it finally reaches the people who paid for it.
