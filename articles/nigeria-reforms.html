Nigeria's Reforms Got the Numbers Right. Why Haven't Nigerians Felt It Yet?

Nigeria's reforms have improved growth, inflation, reserves and government revenue. Yet about 140 million Nigerians are poor, and wages buy less than they did. This piece explains why ordinary citizens have not felt the gains, where the money went, and what the January 2027 election should decide.

By Muiz Olasupo · 24 October 2026 · 3 min read

ShareXLinkedInWhatsApp

A stable economy, a strained household

Nigeria's reforms have got the big numbers right. Growth is up, inflation is down, reserves are rising and governments are collecting far more revenue (NBS; NBS; World Bank; CBN). Yet ordinary Nigerians have not felt it.

Take one everyday measure. In July 2024, when the ₦70,000 minimum wage was set, petrol averaged about ₦770 a litre nationwide, so a month's pay bought about 91 litres (NBS). By May 2026 petrol averaged about ₦1,596, and the same pay bought about 44 (NBS). The minimum wage now buys less than half the petrol it did.

On the national poverty line, the World Bank puts the poverty rate at about 63 percent in 2025, some 140 million people, up from 56 percent in 2023 (World Bank, April 2026). Nigeria has repaired its national accounts. The household account is still in the red.

Why growth has not reached households

Growth of about 4 percent is spread thinly across a fast-growing population, and the World Bank itself calls per capita growth weak (World Bank, October 2026). It has also come mainly from services and industry, while agriculture, which employs more than half of the poor, lagged (World Bank, April 2026).

Prices are the bigger barrier. Lower inflation means prices are rising more slowly, not that they have fallen. Staple food prices faced by the poor rose about fivefold between 2020 and 2024, and poor households spend up to 70 percent of their income on food (World Bank, October 2025; World Bank). Now oil at $110 a barrel has pushed energy inflation up to 10.85 percent (CNBC; NBS).

On the World Bank's stricter $3-a-day line, extreme poverty is still higher in 2028 than it was in 2023.

Where the money went

Removing the petrol subsidy and unifying the exchange rate turned a hidden consumer subsidy into public revenue. Federation revenues rose 69 percent in real terms between 2023 and 2025, and state revenues about 93 percent. The states mostly built things: capital projects grew from 46 to 61 percent of their budgets, while social protection reached only 4.4 percent (World Bank).

That is the gap. Households paid the cost at once, through fuel, transport and food prices. The dividend is taking the long road, through roads and buildings that raise incomes slowly, and only if they are finished. Protection for the poor should have come before the shock, not years after it.

The election test

Nigerians vote for a president on 16 January 2027 (INEC). President Bola Tinubu has ruled out a return of the fuel subsidy (Channels TV). Atiku Abubakar and Seyi Makinde would sell crude more cheaply to local refineries, and Peter Obi says subsidy will return, without yet saying how (Vanguard).

The test for any plan is its design, not its label. A blanket pump subsidy pays most to those who burn most, and cheap crude for refiners gives up revenue the states would otherwise share.

Voters should put three questions to every candidate:

  1. How will you get cash to poor households quickly?
  2. How will you bring down food prices?
  3. How will you make states account for what they built with the windfall?

From stabilisation to development

Stabilisation is a precondition for development, not a substitute for it. Three to four million young Nigerians enter the labour market every year, about one in four young people is in neither work, education nor training, and more than 86 million Nigerians lack electricity (World Bank Country Partnership Framework). None of these numbers moves because reserves rise.

Cheaper money has not helped yet either. The Central Bank cut its policy rate to 23 percent in September, but credit to the private sector fell that month (CBN; The Guardian).

The reforms have bought Nigeria fiscal room and time. Whoever wins in January inherits the dividend. The test is whether it finally reaches the people who paid for it.

Notes on data

Poverty figures after 2018/19 are World Bank estimates and projections, not new household surveys. The national-line estimate (about 63 percent in 2025) and the international extreme-poverty line used in Figure 2 ($3.00 a day, 2021 PPP) measure different thresholds and are not interchangeable. Inflation figures use the rebased consumer price index introduced in 2025, so comparisons with pre-2025 rates mix two series. The petrol comparison uses NBS national average pump prices for July 2024 and May 2026, the latest month published.
