Liquidity Shocks, Fiscal Reforms, and Energy Infrastructure: Evidence on Consumption Smoothing from Nigeria

with Motunrayo Dada, Abiodun Emiola, and Olufemi Saibu

Abstract

The Permanent Income Hypothesis predicts that households absorb transitory shocks through borrowing and saving. We test it with a rare natural experiment: three near-simultaneous shocks in Nigeria during 2023-2024, a currency-redesign cash crisis, the removal of fuel subsidies, and the commissioning of the Dangote Refinery, each activating a distinct smoothing mechanism. The cash crisis delivers our central result. Using difference-in-differences with treatment fixed before the shocks, financially excluded households suffered a consumption cut more than 60 percent larger than the financially included, an order of magnitude beyond estimates from high-income settings. Pre-shock trajectories tracked closely for eight years; the estimate survives selection bounds, holds across the consumption distribution and wealth quartiles, and is largest among constrained households with the most savings, the signature of payment-system failure rather than wealth destruction. We read it as a transaction-technology collapse comparable to India's 2016 demonetization, in which the withdrawal of cash severed excluded households from the market itself. Evidence from the subsidy and refinery shocks is consistent with the model's remaining mechanisms. The policy implication is sharp: financial inclusion is a precondition for welfare-preserving fiscal reform, and reforms enacted amid deep financial fragmentation inflict severe, regressive welfare costs that sequencing could substantially reduce.

Heat, Aging, and Adaptation: Decomposing Climate Vulnerability in U.S. Counties

Sole-authored

Abstract

As climate change raises temperatures and the US elderly population swells from 12 to 17 percent of the population between 2000 and 2024, two forces are pulling American public health in opposite directions. Hotter summers threaten cardiovascular health, especially among older adults whose thermoregulatory systems are weakest, yet decades of air conditioning adoption have steadily reduced the mortality impact of extreme heat. Whether adaptation is outpacing this rapid demographic shift remains an open question. This paper answers it using 25 years of county-level data on age-specific cardiovascular mortality, temperature, and demographics across 3,135 US counties. I exploit within-county weather variation in a triple-difference design that compares how elderly and working-age mortality respond to heat shocks, and how that differential evolves over time. The results are clear: the elderly are adapting. Under the most demanding specification, which absorbs state-by-year-by-age fixed effects, a one-standard-deviation heat shock closes the elderly-young mortality gap by 1.3 deaths per 100,000 more each year than it did at the start of the sample. Adaptation among older Americans has outpaced the mechanical increase in vulnerability from population aging. Critically, the speed of adaptation depends on energy costs. In states where electricity is cheaper, elderly heat vulnerability has fallen faster, pointing to residential air conditioning as the primary protective technology. This finding reframes energy assistance programs such as the Low Income Home Energy Assistance Program: they are not merely antipoverty transfers but function as climate adaptation policy for an aging population.

Fiscal Dominance and Monetary Policy Inertia: Evidence from Nigeria

with Dotun Isenere

Abstract

Nigeria's 1,600 basis point monetary tightening cycle of 2022 to 2024 coincided with a doubling of inflation, raising the question of whether fiscal dominance has rendered the interest rate channel inoperative. This paper measures fiscal encroachment on the central bank as the ratio of net claims on government to GDP and tests it against the monetary policy rate using 179 months of data, Granger causality, out-of-sample forecasting, cointegration analysis, instrumental variables estimation, and local projections. Government credit Granger-causes inflation but not vice versa, while the policy rate follows inflation rather than leading it. Out of sample, the fiscal measure reduces forecast error by a third relative to an autoregressive benchmark; the monetary model performs worse than the benchmark, and adding the policy rate to the fiscal model contributes nothing. A bounds test confirms cointegration, and local projections show that a one-standard-deviation fiscal shock raises inflation by nearly six percentage points within a year, with no reversion. Instrumenting with lagged reserve growth confirms the causal interpretation, and a mediation analysis reveals that the transmission operates through the exchange rate and expectations channels rather than conventional money creation. A structural break test detects an intensification of fiscal dominance following the 2023 reform. These findings imply that monetary tightening alone cannot stabilize prices when the dominant source of inflation is fiscal.

Shadow Economy, Tax Revenue and Income Inequality in Africa: A Mediation Analysis

with Abiodun Emiola and Kazeem Ajide

Abstract

Even though existing studies have extensively investigated the impacts of shadow economic activities and tax revenue on income inequality, the literature overlooks the mediating role of tax revenue in the nexus between the shadow economy and income inequality. In addition, the studies' focus groups have been conducted in high- and middle-income countries. To fill gaps in the existing literature on the nexus between the shadow economy, tax revenue, and income inequality, this study investigates the relationship among the shadow economy, tax revenue, and income inequality. Using an unbalanced panel data set on 48 African countries from 2000 to 2015 and deploying the structural equation modeling (SEM) approach, the empirical results are summarized as follows: (i) for the full sample, shadow economic activities indirectly increase income inequality through decreasing tax revenue. In other words, tax revenue plays a partial mediation role between shadow economic activities and income inequality; and (ii) for the different subregions, tax revenue shows varying mediation effects. For example, the mediation effects of tax revenue in Eastern and Western Africa are similar to those in the full sample of countries. Therefore, based on these findings, we conclude that shadow economic activities are an essential determinant of income inequality. Policy recommendations are put forward.

De-Risking Funding: How Development Finance Institutions (DFIs) Can Attract Institutional Investors to Unlock Capital for Impact Development in Africa

with Habeebullah Asudemade

Abstract

Africa's USD 170-402 billion annual financing gaps for infrastructure and sustainable development, with USD 130-170 billion needed yearly, prompts this study to explore how Development Finance Institutions (DFIs), like Nigeria's Bank of Industry and Development Bank of Nigeria, can use de-risking tools (guarantees, blended finance, political risk insurance, and hedging) to mobilise institutional investors managing USD 1.1-1.8 trillion, yet allocating less than 2% to infrastructure. Using a panel data fixed effects regression for Nigeria, South Africa, Kenya, and Ethiopia (2018-2023), we find de-risking significantly boosts capital, as seen in InfraCredit's ₦158.8 billion guaranteed bonds (2023) and Climate Investor One's USD 462 million at 5-25x leverage. Scalability is limited by regulatory caps and governance issues curbing FDI by 10-15%, and FX volatility raising costs by 5-10%. Policy recommendations include standardizing instruments, reforming pension regulations, expanding hedging via TCX, and adopting inclusive models to align with SDGs (7, 9, 13) and Agenda 2063, offering actionable insights to enhance Nigeria's industrial growth.
